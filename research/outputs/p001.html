Abstract

Whether pretraining scaling is “slowing down” is usually argued from loss curves: a frontier model’s loss is compared with a law fitted to smaller runs. We ask how large a slowdown such a comparison can detect, and give a closed-form answer.

Theory. Express any deviation from the law (a data wall, falling data quality, an exponent break, a worse recipe) as a compute penalty PP: the run at compute CC reaches the loss the law assigns to C/PC/P. Suppose a loss comparison has total log-scale uncertainty ss, made up of forecast and measurement error. Theorem 1 shows that the effective compute it can certify is Ccert(C)=(G/((1+k)R(C)+kE))1/γC_\mathrm{cert}(C)=\big(G/((1+k)R(C)+kE)\big)^{1/\gamma}, with k=ezs−1k=e^{zs}-1. This is strictly bounded by an audit ceiling C†=(G/kE)1/γC^\dagger=(G/kE)^{1/\gamma}, the compute at which the reducible loss equals kEkE. Beyond C†C^\dagger, the undetectable penalty grows in proportion to CC. For forecast-based comparisons, ss cannot fall below the sweep’s standard error of lnÊ\ln\hat E, however precisely the frontier run is measured. The ceiling moves by a factor 101/γ10^{1/\gamma} (about 4×1054\times10^5 at the Chinchilla exponent) for each 10× change in ss. Monte Carlo replications confirm the closed form wherever the noise is independent, and they show where it fails, namely when the correlated noise is estimated from few budgets.

Public data. - For the law fitted to the 240 public Chinchilla runs, sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E) is 0.014–0.020 depending on how the bootstrap resamples. The resulting audit ceiling at 50% power is 1.3×10261.3\times10^{26}–7.6×10267.6\times10^{26} FLOP, which spans the largest known training runs. - Suppose a 5×10265\times10^{26} FLOP run lands exactly on this law’s forecast. A nonparametric bootstrap then certifies only 1.3×10251.3\times10^{25}–3.8×10253.8\times10^{25} FLOP of effective compute at 95% confidence, so a 13–40× compute shortfall would go unseen. - On a second public suite (Gadre et al., 2024), Chinchilla-form fits to runs of at most 412M parameters miss the held-out 6.9B runs by up to 8% in loss. That error amounts to compute-equivalent factors of 0.47–4.5 with the default fit. Across five reasonable fitting choices, the factors run from “below the fitted irreducible loss” up to 6.3.

Design and an alternative test. - In a sweep, sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E) scales as σb1.00K−0.38\sigma_b^{1.00}K^{-0.38}, where σb\sigma_b is the per-budget correlated noise and KK the number of budgets. The reach of the ceiling beyond the top of the sweep is exactly γ−1log10(R(Chi)/kE)\gamma^{-1}\log_{10}(R(C_\mathrm{hi})/kE) decades. - Public anchors put σb\sigma_b between about 4×10−44\times10^{-4} (Chinchilla, between compute bins) and 22–8×10−38\times10^{-3} (Gadre et al., re-tuning warm-up or head count). - At a fixed 240 runs, spreading them over 48 budgets rather than 12 raises the power of the in-sweep break test at δ=0.02\delta=0.02 from 0.09 to 0.43. - Proposition 4 gives an EE-free alternative. Three runs at C0C_0, 10C010C_0 and 100C0100C_0 share one recipe. The test is the gap statistic g2−10−γ̂g1g_2-10^{-\hat\gamma}g_1, which is invariant to EE and to any constant penalty. At the top of a 5×10245\times10^{24}–5×10265\times10^{26} ladder it resolves an exponent loss of δ=0.017\delta=0.017 (50% power) when the per-run noise is 10−410^{-4}. It resolves only δ=0.043\delta=0.043 at 3×10−43\times10^{-4} and nothing below γ/2\gamma/2 at 10−310^{-3}.

Answer to the research question. Public loss data cannot answer whether pretraining scaling has slowed at the frontier. This is not because the data show no slowdown. It is because, against the best public law, slowdowns worth tens of times in compute are invisible at 5×10265\times10^{26} FLOP. Matched contrasts that cancel EE (A/B runs at one scale, or one-recipe ladders) avoid the ceiling, but they need per-run noise near 10−410^{-4}. The same arithmetic governs the “next growth point”: the levers people debate are compute multipliers, and a 2× lever moves frontier log-loss by only 0.0033. The growth points can therefore be measured only by matched contrasts, not by comparing frontier loss with a law. All numbers come from our CPU code or from cited sources.

1 Introduction

Since 2024, claims that “pretraining scaling is hitting a wall” have rested on benchmark plateaus, reports of disappointing large runs, and the shift of compute toward reinforcement learning and inference-time scaling (Snell et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025). The measurement literature, meanwhile, describes pretraining loss with saturating power laws whose exponents do not change (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Besiroglu et al., 2024; Porian et al., 2024). The usual way to settle the disagreement is a forecast check: fit a law on small runs, extrapolate, and ask whether the big run lands on the curve (for example Gadre et al., 2024). A run that lands on the curve is read as “scaling continues”, and one that lands above it as “scaling is slowing”.

This paper asks a simple question about that check: how large a slowdown can it see? The answer is less reassuring than the practice suggests, and it has a clean form.

Near the irreducible loss EE, a large loss in compute produces only a tiny change in loss. At the Chinchilla exponent, a run that wastes half its compute at 5×10265\times10^{26} FLOP has a log-loss 0.0033 higher than forecast [computed, Exp. 20]. The forecast, however, has an uncertainty of its own, and as C→∞C\to\infty that uncertainty tends to the uncertainty of lnÊ\ln\hat E. Neither more frontier compute nor more precise frontier evaluation removes it. The two facts combine into a hard ceiling on the effective compute that any forecast check can certify.

Contributions.

  1. Compute-equivalent resolution and the audit ceiling (§3, Theorem 1). Every deviation from the law can be written as a compute penalty P(C)P(C). We give the minimum detectable penalty in closed form, prove that the certifiable effective compute is bounded by C†C^\dagger (where R(C†)=kER(C^\dagger)=kE), and show that the undetectable penalty grows in proportion to CC beyond C†C^\dagger. The ceiling has elasticity −1/γ≈−5.6-1/\gamma\approx-5.6 in ss. For forecast checks, ss is bounded below by sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E). As a corollary, at matched compute efficiency every break mechanism is exactly equally detectable by a residual check. Monte Carlo replications validate the closed form and show where it breaks down (§5.1).
  2. The audit ceiling of public scaling laws (§5.2–5.3). For the public Chinchilla law, C†C^\dagger is 1.31.3–7.6×10267.6\times10^{26} FLOP. A nonparametric certification bound shows that a 5×10265\times10^{26} FLOP run landing on the forecast certifies only 1/13 to 1/40 of its compute. Real out-of-sample forecasts on the Gadre et al. (2024) suite show that loss errors of a few per cent at about one decade of extrapolation are multi-× errors in compute.
  3. A design law with empirical noise anchors (§5.4).   4. An EE-free ladder test (§3.4, §5.5). We give a three-run statistic that is invariant to EE and to constant penalties and has a closed-form resolution, validated by Monte Carlo. It moves the bottleneck from the sweep’s knowledge of EE to frontier per-run noise.
  5. Retained, condensed results. These are the calibrated, cluster-robust re-analysis of the Chinchilla runs (§5.6) and the audit of data-wall milestones across 2023–2026 functional forms (§5.7).

2 Related Work

Scaling laws. Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) established power laws in parameters, data and compute, and Hoffmann et al. introduced the form L=E+AN−α+BD−βL=E+AN^{-\alpha}+BD^{-\beta}. Besiroglu et al. (2024) reconstructed the Chinchilla data from the published figure and showed that the original confidence intervals were implausibly narrow; we use their released data. Pearce & Song (2024) and Porian et al. (2024) reconcile Kaplan and Chinchilla through accounting and tuning choices at small scale. Caballero et al. (2023) propose broken power laws for extrapolating across regimes.

Forecasting large runs from small ones. Gadre et al. (2024) build a 104-model testbed (0.011B–6.9B parameters) and predict the loss of over-trained and larger runs from experiments that use about 300× less compute, with their own scaling-law form; their models and losses are public, and we reuse them (§5.3). Choshen et al. (2025) analyse nearly 500 models and show that scaling-law estimates are noisy and sensitive to individual runs. Ruan et al. (2024) fit observational laws across public model families. None of these works expresses forecast uncertainty in compute-equivalent terms, or derives the ceiling that the uncertainty of EE imposes. Our Theorem 1 is elementary, but we have not found it, or the audit-ceiling consequence, stated in the scaling-law literature.

Compute-equivalent accounting. It is common to measure algorithmic and data improvements as “effective compute” multipliers, as in the economic argument of Lu (2026) that sustained progress requires repeated efficiency doublings. We turn the multiplier view into a statement about measurability: how large a multiplier a given loss comparison can resolve.

Data constraints. Muennighoff et al. (2023) fitted a law for repeated data, with decaying value of repeated tokens and excess parameters. Hernandez et al. (2022) and Xue et al. (2023) document the costs of repetition. Several 2025–26 papers revise that law:

  • Lovelace et al. (2026) propose additive overfitting penalties and release their fits.
  • Xu et al. (2026) argue that additive Chinchilla-style laws are misspecified in the data-constrained regime and propose a coupled law, SoftQ.
  • Bryant & Liu (2026) study compute-to-performance conversion under data constraints.
  • Qin et al. (2026) introduce a token-effectiveness function.
  • Kim et al. (2025) and Improving Data-Constrained Scaling with Hyperparameter Transfer and Ensembling (arXiv:2610.37842) find large gains from regularisation, ensembling and hyperparameter transfer.

Villalobos et al. (2024) estimate the stock of public text, and Sevilla et al. (2024) assess whether runs of 2×10292\times10^{29} FLOP are feasible by 2030.

Noise in training runs. van der Wal et al. (2025) find pretraining largely stable across seeds up to 410M parameters in PolyPythias. Seed variance at frontier scale is not public, so we treat noise as a parameter and anchor its correlated part with public sweeps (§5.4).

Corollary 2 (mechanism-agnostic detectability). 3.3 Where the theorem is approximate

The theorem uses a Gaussian approximation for Δ\Delta and a known ss. In practice ss is estimated: by bootstrap or the delta method for the sweep part, and from replicate runs for the frontier part. §5.1 measures the consequences. When the correlated per-budget noise is estimated from only 12 budgets, the plug-in test is anti-conservative.

5.7 Data-wall milestones and mechanisms (Exps. 4, 10, 11; condensed)

The form matters more than the constants. Under the Muennighoff et al. law with consistent UNU_N, compute efficiency falls to 50% at 71×Cwall71\times C_\mathrm{wall} (where repetition starts), identically for every UU (Prop. 3′). In revision 1, Table 4 / Exp. 4 gave 74–75×. That figure used the linear threshold kN=0.051k_N=0.051, rounded, whereas the consistent constant is c=(αA/βB)1/α=0.050c=(\alpha A/\beta B)^{1/\alpha}=0.050; the two agree once the rounding is removed.

Under the additive-penalty laws of Lovelace et al. (2026), computed from their released fits, the same milestone settles at 28–36× for U≥1010U\ge10^{10}. Their effective-data refit puts it at 1,000× (standard arm) or 7,822× (weight decay 1.0) [computed, Exp. 10].

All values at U≥1010U\ge10^{10} extrapolate fits made on 50M–6B unique tokens by 2–6 decades. The “hard-wall” values of the additive forms are a consequence of extrapolating a penalty that is linear in N/UDN/U_D; they are not established properties at scale.

Xu et al. (2026) argue that additive Chinchilla-style forms are misspecified in exactly this compute-rich, data-constrained regime and propose a coupled law, SoftQ. We have not implemented SoftQ, because it would require its fitted parameters and a check of our implementation against theirs. Its milestones are therefore not in our table. Its existence widens, rather than narrows, the model uncertainty we report.

Mechanisms. By Corollary 2, a unique-data cap, a decline in data quality and a non-data exponent break are equally detectable by a residual check at matched efficiency. Revision 1’s comparison of in-sweep power across the three mechanisms did not match their magnitudes (η=0.73\eta=0.73 for the exponent break against 0.97 and 0.96 for the data mechanisms at 3Cx3C_x), so it is withdrawn. What separates them is the response to the data lever itself. The matched data-doubling gap grows without bound under a cap, saturates near 5×10−45\times10^{-4} under quality decline, and is zero under a non-data break (Exp. 11). This experiment is what Muennighoff et al. (2023), Lovelace et al. (2026) and Kim et al. (2025) already run in other forms; we claim no novelty for it.

6 Discussion

Is pretraining scaling slowing down? In the mechanical sense, yes, by construction, at 0.66× per decade. In the genuine sense, the public evidence is uninformative at the frontier, and Theorem 1 says how uninformative. Against the best public law, a 5×10265\times10^{26} FLOP run that lands on the curve certifies at most 1/13 to 1/40 of its compute (Table 3).

“Frontier models land on the scaling curve” is therefore weak evidence that scaling continues, and “frontier models land somewhat above the curve” is weak evidence that it is slowing. Neither statement can be made at a resolution better than an order of magnitude in compute, unless the sweep pins down EE far better than any public sweep does.

Why this matters beyond our data. The ceiling is structural. It exists because loss saturates at EE, and its location depends only on how well EE is known. With γ≈0.18\gamma\approx0.18, every 10× improvement in sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E) moves the ceiling by 5.6 decades. Conversely, a lab whose internal sweep has sd(lnÊ)≈10−3\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E)\approx10^{-3}, which needs a fixed recipe and many budgets (Table 5), can audit far beyond today’s frontier. This is a concrete, measurable target. We recommend that forecast checks report C†C^\dagger, or the certification bound, alongside the residual.

Where is the next growth point? The levers debated as successors to pretraining scale (data quality, repetition and regularisation, synthetic data, algorithmic efficiency, RL and inference-time compute) are compute multipliers. At 5×10265\times10^{26} FLOP a 2× multiplier moves pretraining log-loss by 0.0033, and its signal shrinks by about 10−γ10^{-\gamma} per decade (0.0020 at 102810^{28}; Exp. 20).

Two consequences follow:

  • Frontier pretraining loss compared with a law cannot rank these levers, because they lie below its resolution.
  • Matched contrasts, such as A/B runs at one scale, data-doubling pairs and one-recipe ladders, can rank them, because they cancel EE (Corollary 3, Proposition 4).

Our analysis does not say which lever wins. It says how the question has to be measured, and that conclusions drawn from frontier loss alone, in either direction, are at present below the resolution of the available public data. For data-constrained levers, the timing of the wall differs by more than two orders of magnitude across published functional forms (§5.7). Resolving that is a small-scale research target.

Recommendations.

    1. Report the compute-equivalent resolution (PminP_{\min} or the certification bound) of every forecast check, not only the loss residual.
    1. Treat sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E) as the key output of a sweep. Use a fixed recipe and many budgets, and calibrate with cluster bootstraps.
    1. For exponent slowdowns at the frontier, use one-recipe ladders and measure per-run noise.
    1. For growth levers, use matched contrasts at the target scale.
    1. Report the support of the data and the sensitivity to excluded runs.

6.1 Falsifiable predictions

  • P1 (ceiling scaling). In any controlled sweep, the empirical forecast error at C≫ChiC\gg C_\mathrm{hi} approaches sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E), and the reach follows Theorem 1(iii). Falsified if held-out runs far beyond the sweep consistently show forecast errors much smaller than sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E), which would indicate that the bootstrap overstates EE-uncertainty.
  • P2 (budget allocation). At fixed run count, sd(lnÊ)\mathrm{sd}(\ln\hat E) falls roughly as K−0.38K^{-0.38} up to about 48 budgets when per-budget offsets dominate. Falsified by a re-split of a public sweep, or a new one, that shows no gain.
  • P3 (ladder invariance). For one-recipe ladders, g2/g1g_2/g_1 is independent of recipe quality (constant penalties) to within noise. A sustained g2/g1>10−γg_2/g_1>10^{-\gamma} across generations would be direct evidence of an exponent slowdown.
  • P4 (absorption). Refitting the no-break law to data with a break raises Ê\hat E (Exp. 6a). Suites with more repeated data therefore give higher Ê\hat E at equal compute.
  • P5 (data-wall self-similarity). Under Muennighoff-type laws, efficiency milestones collapse against C/UτC/U^{\tau} with τ\tau estimated in the sweep. Additive forms drift at small UU.

7 Limitations

  • Functional form. Theorem 1 assumes the frontier is E+GC−γE+GC^{-\gamma}. If the true curve does not saturate at a constant EE (for example, if the law changes form), the ceiling is a statement about the fitted law, not about nature. That is exactly the situation a forecast check is used in, but it is still an assumption.
  • Gaussian approximation and estimated ss. The closed form is validated under independent noise. With per-budget noise estimated from 12 budgets, plug-in tests over-reject (size up to 0.105). Our nonparametric certification bounds avoid the Gaussian approximation, but they inherit the bootstrap’s assumptions, including exchangeability of runs or clusters.
  • Public data. The Chinchilla runs are digitised from a figure with 94 colour levels, and their ceiling moves by up to three orders of magnitude when five runs are restored. The Gadre suite has only four small model sizes, and its 6.9B runs used a different recipe. We fit the Chinchilla form to it, not the authors’ law. Neither suite reaches 102210^{22} FLOP.
  • Noise anchors. The correlated-noise anchors come from hyper-parameter sweeps and residual decompositions at small scale. They indicate magnitudes but are not frontier measurements. Frontier seed and recipe variance is not public, so the ladder test’s feasibility is conditional on it.
  • Ladder test. Proposition 4 assumes compute-optimal rungs with a shared recipe. Over-trained ladders follow the compute-optimal form only approximately, and per-rung recipe changes inflate the size sharply (0.31–0.41 at offsets of 10−310^{-3}).
  • Scope. We study pretraining cross-entropy on a fixed evaluation distribution. Downstream capabilities, RL and inference-time scaling are outside our quantitative scope. Our statement about them is only that loss-based measurement of their compute-equivalent value is limited in the way described.
  • No new training runs. P1–P5 are predictions, not results.

8 Conclusion

A loss curve can hide a large slowdown. The amount follows from a short theorem. The certifiable effective compute of a loss comparison saturates at the compute where the reducible loss equals (ezs−1)E(e^{zs}-1)E. For forecast checks, ss cannot fall below the sweep’s uncertainty in lnÊ\ln\hat E. For the best public scaling law, this ceiling lies at about 102610^{26}–102710^{27} FLOP, right at today’s frontier, and a run landing on the forecast there certifies only 1/13 to 1/40 of its compute. Public data therefore cannot say whether pretraining scaling has slowed. Matched contrasts that cancel EE can, given per-run noise near 10−410^{-4}, and the same contrasts are the right instrument for measuring the next growth levers.
