## Abstract

Continual learners that rehearse old data, and even learners trained on the full union of old and new data, show a *stability gap*: as soon as training on a new task starts, accuracy on old tasks drops sharply and then partly or fully recovers (De Lange et al., 2023). The transient matters for any learner that is evaluated while it trains, and it has so far been explained only empirically. We give an exact account of it in the simplest setting where it can occur, overparameterized linear regression with replay weight $\rho$. The joint objective there is convex and has a solution with zero loss on both tasks, so terminal forgetting is zero by construction and any forgetting is transient. We prove the following. (i) The old-task loss along the gradient-flow path has a closed form; for rank-one tasks the gap depth is $G=\tfrac12 a c^2 s^2 (1-r)^2 r^{2r/(1-r)}$, with $r$ the ratio of the two Hessian eigenvalues. (ii) The first step of forgetting is reduced by replay only by the factor $(1-\rho)^2$. (iii) A gap occurs for some initial error if and only if the new-task Hessian fails to leave the old task's null space invariant. (iv) As $\rho\to0$ the gap depth is bounded below by a quantity that tends to the forgetting incurred *without* replay. (v) The recovery time is $\Theta(1/\rho)$: we give explicit lower and upper bounds, and $\rho\,T_\varepsilon(\rho)$ converges to a constant set by a reduced "slow" system. (vi) Any method whose path stays in the old task's null space has no gap; exact Gauss–Newton on the replay objective and gradient projection are two such methods, and the Gauss–Newton route is ill-conditioned as $\rho\to0$. (vii) Mini-batch SGD with replay cannot reduce the expected gap at a fixed step size, and its first-step law gains a noise term that scales as $(1-\rho)/B$ instead of $(1-\rho)^2$. (viii) With a finite replay buffer the terminal forgetting is a $\rho$-independent constant, and the transient sits on top of it. Simulations agree with the closed forms (relative error below $3\times10^{-13}$ against an independent ODE integrator), the bounds hold on every instance tested and are loose by two orders of magnitude, and $\rho T_{0.1}$ is within 0.8% of the predicted constant for $\rho\le10^{-3}$. In a 1,210-parameter network trained on sklearn digits, depth is as insensitive to small replay weights as in the linear model (55–74% of the no-replay damage remains at $\rho=0.01$). The $(1-\rho)^2$ law holds at the first step, where it is essentially Taylor's theorem, and holds over 10 steps only for $\rho\le0.03$. A flow-like damped Gauss–Newton update reduces the gap relative to GD at $\rho\in\{0.1,0.3\}$; at matched new-task progress (6 seeds, paired 95% confidence intervals) the reduction is 34–75% depending on scenario, $\rho$ and the progress level, and lowering GD's step size tenfold changes its gap by about 1%. Exact elimination does not transfer to the network, and neither does the damping-scaling prediction. We state what the linear theory does and does not explain, and give falsifiable predictions for larger models with the caveats our own negative results impose.

## 中文摘要

持续学习中的"稳定性缺口"(stability gap)指：模型一开始学习新任务，旧任务性能就骤降，随后部分或完全恢复。这一现象在经验回放乃至联合训练中都会出现(De Lange 等，2023)，但至今只有经验性解释。本文在最简单的可出现该现象的设定(回放权重为 $\rho$ 的过参数化线性回归)中给出精确理论。该设定的联合目标是凸的，并且存在同时完美拟合两个任务的解，因此终态遗忘按构造为零，一切遗忘都是暂态的。我们证明：(i) 梯度流路径上的旧任务损失有闭式解，秩一任务的缺口深度为 $G=\tfrac12 a c^2 s^2(1-r)^2r^{2r/(1-r)}$；(ii) 回放只把第一步的遗忘降低 $(1-\rho)^2$ 倍；(iii) 当且仅当新任务 Hessian 不保持旧任务零空间不变时，存在会产生缺口的初始误差；(iv) $\rho\to0$ 时缺口深度的下界趋于无回放时的遗忘量；(v) 恢复时间为 $\Theta(1/\rho)$：我们给出显式的上下界，并且 $\rho\,T_\varepsilon(\rho)$ 收敛到由约化"慢系统"决定的常数；(vi) 任何路径始终留在旧任务零空间内的方法都没有缺口，对回放目标做精确 Gauss–Newton 以及梯度投影是其中两例，而 Gauss–Newton 途径在 $\rho\to0$ 时是病态的；(vii) 在固定步长下，带回放的小批量 SGD 不能降低期望缺口，其首步律多出一个按 $(1-\rho)/B$ (而非 $(1-\rho)^2$)缩放的噪声项；(viii) 回放缓冲区有限时，终态遗忘是与 $\rho$ 无关的常数，暂态叠加其上。数值实验与闭式解一致(相对独立 ODE 积分器的相对误差低于 $3\times10^{-13}$)；各个界在全部实例上成立，但偏松约两个数量级；当 $\rho\le10^{-3}$ 时 $\rho T_{0.1}$ 与预测常数相差不超过 0.8%。在 sklearn digits 上训练的 1,210 参数网络中，缺口深度对小回放权重同样不敏感($\rho=0.01$ 时仍保留无回放损伤的 55–74%)；$(1-\rho)^2$ 律在第一步成立(此时它基本就是泰勒定理)，在 10 步内仅对 $\rho\le0.03$ 成立。在 $\rho\in\{0.1,0.3\}$ 时，类流(小步长)阻尼 Gauss–Newton 相对 GD 减小缺口；在新任务进度匹配的比较中(6 个随机种子，配对 95% 置信区间)，减小幅度为 34–75%(取决于场景、$\rho$ 与进度水平)，而把 GD 步长缩小十倍只使其缺口改变约 1%。精确消除缺口的结论以及阻尼缩放的预测都不能推广到该网络。本文说明线性理论能解释与不能解释的部分，并在我们自己的负面结果所要求的保留条件下，给出可在更大模型上证伪的预测。

## 1 Introduction

Whether a model can keep learning without catastrophic forgetting (McCloskey & Cohen, 1989; French, 1999) is usually judged by *end-of-task* accuracy. The model is trained on task 2 and then evaluated on task 1. By that measure, rehearsal (replay) of old data is one of the most reliable remedies known (Rolnick et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024). Continual *evaluation*, which measures old-task performance at every iteration, gives a different picture. De Lange, van de Ven and Tuytelaars (2023) found that replay, regularization and other methods all show a large but transient loss of old-task performance immediately after a task switch, followed by recovery. They called this the **stability gap**. Later work found it in class-incremental learning with pre-trained models (Harun & Kanan, 2024), in joint incremental training on *homogeneous* tasks drawn from a single distribution (Kamath et al., 2024), in continual pre-training of language models (Gupta et al., 2023), and concentrated largely in the linear classification head (Łapacz et al., 2025). A system that serves predictions while it learns is exposed during the trough, and the trough is invisible to end-of-task metrics.

Existing explanations are qualitative. De Lange et al. (2023) note that the gradient of a stability term is zero at the old optimum, so at a task switch only the plasticity gradient acts. Hess et al. (2023) argue that the continual-learning community should ask not only *what* to optimize but *how*. Kamath et al. (2024) observe empirically that a low-loss linear path to the next minimum exists and that SGD does not follow it. Remedies have been engineered empirically: layerwise proximal updates (Yoo et al., 2024), changes to the replay schedule and head (Harun & Kanan, 2024), and gain modulation (Rodriguez-Garcia et al., 2025). To our knowledge there is no model in which the stability gap can be computed exactly, its existence characterized, and its depth and duration derived as functions of the replay ratio. The linear-regression theory of forgetting (Evron et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023; Goldfarb & Hand, 2023; Mahdaviyeh et al., 2025) analyses the *endpoints* of sequential training, not the path between them.

**This paper.** We study the *trajectory* of gradient-based training on a replay objective $(1-\rho)L_2+\rho L_1$, starting from a minimizer of $L_1$, in overparameterized linear regression where both tasks can be fit exactly. This is the most favourable possible setting for replay. The objective is convex, the terminal old-task loss is zero for every $\rho>0$, and any rise in $L_1$ is therefore purely transient. If a stability gap appears here, it cannot be blamed on non-convexity, insufficient buffer size, representation drift, or an unreachable joint optimum.

**What is and is not new.** The conceptual messages are not ours. That the replay gradient vanishes at the old optimum is De Lange et al.'s argument; that the gap survives joint training is due to Kamath et al. and Hess et al.; that a low-loss straight path exists is Kamath et al.'s observation; that the optimizer matters is Hess et al.'s thesis. The mathematics is elementary: an eigendecomposition, a Taylor expansion, an invariant-subspace argument and a Duhamel bound, and non-monotone transients of a quadratic form along $e^{-Ht}$ are classical in two-timescale linear systems. What we add is a model in which those messages become exact statements with constants, and four quantitative results that we have not found elsewhere: the $(1-\rho)^2$ first-step law and its SGD correction, the existence criterion, the small-$\rho$ depth bound, and two-sided $\Theta(1/\rho)$ bounds on recovery time with the limiting constant.

**Contributions.**

1. **Exact path formula and closed form (§3–4, Prop. 1, Thm. 1).** $L_1(t)=\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}e^{-H_\rho t}e_0\|^2$ with $H_\rho=(1-\rho)\Sigma_2+\rho\Sigma_1$. For rank-one tasks the gap depth and peak time are closed-form.
2. **A first-step law (Thm. 2).** $L_1(t)=\tfrac12(1-\rho)^2t^2\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2e_0\|^2+O(t^3)$, exact for one GD step. Replay reduces the first step of forgetting only by $(1-\rho)^2$.
3. **An existence criterion (Thm. 3).** A gap occurs for some admissible initial error if and only if $\Sigma_2$ does not leave $\ker\Sigma_1$ invariant.
4. **Small replay does not shrink the gap (Thm. 4).** $\sqrt{2G(\rho)}\ge\sqrt{2F_\infty}-O(\rho\log(1/\rho))$, where $F_\infty$ is the terminal forgetting *without* replay.
5. **Recovery takes time $\Theta(1/\rho)$ (Thm. 7, Prop. 8).** For the last time $T_\varepsilon$ at which $L_1\ge\varepsilon F_\infty$ we prove $c_1/\rho\le T_\varepsilon\le c_2/\rho$ with explicit constants, and $\rho T_\varepsilon\to T^\star_\varepsilon$, the exit time of a reduced gradient flow on $\ker\Sigma_2$.
6. **Straight paths have no gap (Thm. 6).** Any trajectory that stays in $\ker\Sigma_1$ has $L_1\equiv0$. Exact Gauss–Newton on the replay objective and projected gradient are two instances. We do not claim the joint-curvature metric is privileged.
7. **SGD and finite buffers (Thm. 9, Prop. 10).** Exact second-moment dynamics for mini-batch replay SGD; the expected gap is never smaller than GD's at the same step size; the first-step law acquires a $(1-\rho)/B$ noise term. With a buffer of $m<n_1$ samples the terminal forgetting is a $\rho$-independent constant.
8. **A nonlinear check with matched comparisons (§6) and conditional predictions (§7).** We report which predictions transfer to a small network, with paired confidence intervals, and which do not.

The contribution is a mechanistic theory of one phenomenon in a deliberately simple model, together with evidence about which parts survive nonlinearity. It is not a new continual-learning method.

## 2 Related Work

**Catastrophic forgetting and replay.** Forgetting under sequential training was identified by McCloskey & Cohen (1989) and reviewed by French (1999). Mitigation families include regularization such as EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), gradient projection such as GEM and OGD (Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, 2017; Farajtabar et al., 2020), and replay (Rolnick et al., 2019). Wang et al. (2024) give a recent survey, and van de Ven et al. (2022) define the task-, domain- and class-incremental scenarios used in our experiments.

**The stability gap.** De Lange et al. (2023) introduced continual evaluation and identified the gap. Kamath et al. (2024) showed that it persists in joint incremental training of homogeneous tasks and that a low-loss linear path to the next minimum exists. Harun & Kanan (2024) studied it with pre-trained networks and proposed mitigations. Łapacz et al. (2025) located most of it in the linear head. Earlier, Caccia et al. (2022) traced abrupt representation change at task boundaries under experience replay to new-class gradients disrupting old representations. Gupta et al. (2023) observed an analogous loss spike when continuing to pre-train language models. Rodriguez-Garcia et al. (2025) attenuate it with a two-timescale gain mechanism, and Yoo et al. (2024) with a proximal-point replay optimizer. Hess et al. (2023) argued for studying *how* continual objectives are optimized. All of these are empirical or conceptual. Our results put several of their observations into one solvable model.

**Theory of forgetting in linear and kernel models.** Evron et al. (2022) computed exact forgetting for sequences of overparameterized linear regression tasks trained to convergence, and related it to principal angles between task subspaces. Lin et al. (2023) derived expected forgetting and generalization error under Gaussian features. Goldfarb & Hand (2023) analysed tasks related by random orthogonal transformations. Mahdaviyeh et al. (2025) showed that replay of a subset of old samples can *increase* terminal forgetting in overparameterized linear regression, and that forgetting can be non-monotone in the number of replayed samples. Doan et al. (2021) analysed forgetting through the NTK overlap matrix, and Lee et al. (2021) used a teacher–student model to show that intermediate task similarity is worst. All of these study the converged solution of each phase. We study the *transient within a phase* under a joint (replay) objective. In that regime the endpoint analyses predict zero forgetting, yet the path forgets. Our no-replay limit ($\rho=0$) recovers the endpoint forgetting of Evron et al. (Prop. 1), and our finite-buffer endpoint (Prop. 10) is the quantity Mahdaviyeh et al. study.

**Curvature-aware optimization in continual learning.** Natural-gradient and Kronecker-factored curvature (Martens & Grosse, 2015) underlie Fisher-based continual methods such as NCL (Kao et al., 2021), which casts gradient projection as a Fisher trust region. Mirzadeh et al. (2020) relate forgetting to the old task's Hessian along the displacement. Theorem 6 shows that in the linear model both the projection route (OGD) and the joint-curvature route produce paths that the old task cannot see.

## 3 Setting

There are two regression tasks with design matrices $X_k\in\mathbb R^{n_k\times d}$ and second-moment matrices $\Sigma_k=X_k^\top X_k/n_k\succeq0$, for $k=1,2$. We assume **joint realizability**: there is $w^\star$ with $X_kw^\star=y_k$ for both $k$. This holds generically when $n_1+n_2\le d$. The losses are $L_k(w)=\tfrac1{2n_k}\|X_kw-y_k\|^2=\tfrac12(w-w^\star)^\top\Sigma_k(w-w^\star)$. Phase 1 ends at any interpolator $\bar w_1$ of task 1, so $L_1(\bar w_1)=0$. Phase 2 minimizes the **replay objective**

$$L_\rho(w)=(1-\rho)L_2(w)+\rho L_1(w),\qquad \rho\in[0,1),$$

where $\rho$ is the effective replay weight (the fraction of each batch's loss carried by old data). It is optimized by gradient flow $\dot w=-\nabla L_\rho(w)$, or by gradient descent with step $\eta$. Define the error $e=w-w^\star$, its initial value $e_0=\bar w_1-w^\star\in\ker\Sigma_1$, and the joint Hessian $H_\rho=(1-\rho)\Sigma_2+\rho\Sigma_1$. Norms of matrices are operator norms, and $\lambda^+_{\min}$ denotes the smallest nonzero eigenvalue.

**Definition (stability gap).** The *gap depth* is $G(\rho)=\sup_{t\ge0}L_1(w(t))-L_1(w(\infty))$. For realizable replay ($\rho>0$) we show $L_1(w(\infty))=0$, so $G=\sup_tL_1$. We also use the *no-replay forgetting curve* $F(\tau)=\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}e^{-\Sigma_2\tau}e_0\|^2$ and its limit $F_\infty=\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}P_{\ker\Sigma_2}e_0\|^2$, which is the endpoint forgetting studied by Evron et al. (2022). The *recovery time* at level $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ is the last time the old-task loss is at least $\varepsilon F_\infty$: $T_\varepsilon(\rho)=\sup\{t:L_1(t)\ge\varepsilon F_\infty\}$.

## 4 Theory

All proofs are elementary. We give them in full because the results are only useful if they are checkable.

**Proposition 1 (path and endpoints).** Under gradient flow, $e(t)=e^{-H_\rho t}e_0$ and $L_1(t)=\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}e^{-H_\rho t}e_0\|^2$. Moreover $L_1(0)=0$. For $\rho\in(0,1)$, $L_1(\infty)=0$. For $\rho=0$, $L_1(\infty)=F_\infty$.

*Proof.* $\nabla L_\rho(w)=H_\rho e$, so $\dot e=-H_\rho e$. As $t\to\infty$, $e^{-H_\rho t}\to P_{\ker H_\rho}$. For $\rho\in(0,1)$, $H_\rho$ is a positive combination of PSD matrices, so $\ker H_\rho=\ker\Sigma_1\cap\ker\Sigma_2$ and $\Sigma_1P_{\ker H_\rho}=0$. For $\rho=0$, $\ker H_0=\ker\Sigma_2$. $\square$

Replay with any positive weight therefore eliminates *terminal* forgetting in this model. Everything that follows concerns the transient.

**Theorem 1 (closed form for rank-one tasks).** Let $\Sigma_1=a\,uu^\top$ and $\Sigma_2=b\,vv^\top$ with unit vectors at angle $\theta\in(0,\pi/2)$, working in $\mathrm{span}\{u,v\}$, and let $e_0=c\,u_\perp$. Let $0<\lambda_1<\lambda_2$ be the eigenvalues of $H_\rho$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $q_1,q_2$, and write $s=(u^\top q_1)(q_1^\top u_\perp)$ and $r=\lambda_1/\lambda_2$. Then for $\rho\in(0,1)$
$$L_1(t)=\tfrac12ac^2s^2\left(e^{-\lambda_1t}-e^{-\lambda_2t}\right)^2,\qquad t^\star=\frac{\ln(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1},\qquad G=\tfrac12ac^2s^2(1-r)^2r^{2r/(1-r)}.$$
As $\rho\to0^+$, $G\to\tfrac12ac^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta=F_\infty$ and $t^\star\to\infty$. At $\rho=0$ itself there is no interior peak: $L_1$ increases monotonically to $F_\infty$.

*Proof.* $u^\top e^{-Ht}u_\perp=\sum_ie^{-\lambda_it}(u^\top q_i)(q_i^\top u_\perp)$. Since $u\perp u_\perp$, the two coefficients sum to $u^\top u_\perp=0$, so they are $s$ and $-s$. Maximizing $(e^{-\lambda_1t}-e^{-\lambda_2t})^2$ gives $t^\star$ and the value $r^{r/(1-r)}(1-r)$. As $\rho\to0$, $q_2\to v$, $q_1\to v_\perp$ and $r\to0$, so $r^{2r/(1-r)}\to1$ and $s^2\to\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta$. $\square$

The gap is the difference of a fast exponential (the new-task mode, rate $\lambda_2\approx b$) and a slow one (the replay-restored mode, rate $\lambda_1=O(\rho)$). As in the endpoint theory (Evron et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2021), the dependence on task angle is non-monotone and largest at intermediate similarity.

**Theorem 2 (first-step law).** For any $\rho\in[0,1)$,
$$L_1(t)=\tfrac12(1-\rho)^2t^2\,\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2e_0\|^2+O(t^3).$$
For one GD step of size $\eta$, $L_1(w_1)=\tfrac12\eta^2(1-\rho)^2\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2e_0\|^2$ exactly.

*Proof.* $\Sigma_1^{1/2}e^{-Ht}e_0=\Sigma_1^{1/2}(e_0-tHe_0)+O(t^2)$. Since $\Sigma_1e_0=0$, $He_0=(1-\rho)\Sigma_2e_0$ and $\Sigma_1^{1/2}e_0=0$. For GD, $e_1=(I-\eta H)e_0$ and the same cancellation is exact. $\square$

The replay gradient $\rho\Sigma_1e$ vanishes at the old optimum, so replay cannot oppose the first step; it only removes the factor $(1-\rho)$ from the new-task push. This is a quantitative form of the zero-stability-gradient argument of De Lange et al. (2023). The statement is about the leading order only. From the second step on, the replay term is active, and the normalized curve $L_1(k)/(1-\rho)^2$ depends on $\rho$; §6 measures how quickly.

**Theorem 3 (existence criterion).** The following are equivalent: (a) for some (equivalently, every) $\rho\in[0,1)$, $L_1(t)=0$ for all $t\ge0$ and all $e_0\in\ker\Sigma_1$; (b) $\Sigma_2\ker\Sigma_1\subseteq\ker\Sigma_1$; (c) $\Sigma_2P_1=P_1\Sigma_2$, where $P_1$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\mathrm{range}\,\Sigma_1$. In particular, $[\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2]=0$ implies there is never a gap. Conversely, for a specific $e_0$, $\Sigma_1\Sigma_2e_0\ne0$ implies $G(\rho)>0$ for every $\rho\in[0,1)$.

*Proof.* (b)⇒(a): $\Sigma_1$ annihilates $\ker\Sigma_1$, so $H_\rho$ maps $\ker\Sigma_1$ into itself, and so does $e^{-H_\rho t}$. Hence $\Sigma_1e(t)=0$. (a)⇒(b): by Theorem 2, $L_1\equiv0$ forces $\Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2e_0=0$, so $\Sigma_1\Sigma_2e_0=0$ for all $e_0\in\ker\Sigma_1$. (b)⇔(c): a symmetric matrix leaves a subspace invariant if and only if it leaves the orthogonal complement invariant, which holds if and only if it commutes with the projector. The last claim follows from Theorem 2 because the $t^2$ coefficient is positive. $\square$

**Theorem 4 (small replay does not shrink the gap).** For $\rho\in(0,1)$ and every $t\ge0$,
$$\sqrt{2L_1(t)}\;\ge\;\sqrt{2F((1-\rho)t)}-\rho t\,\|\Sigma_1\|^{3/2}\|e_0\|,\tag{4.1}$$
hence $\sqrt{2G(\rho)}\ge\sup_{\tau\ge0}\big[\sqrt{2F(\tau)}-\tfrac{\rho\tau}{1-\rho}\|\Sigma_1\|^{3/2}\|e_0\|\big]$. Let $\mu=\lambda^+_{\min}(\Sigma_2)$. Taking $\tau=\log(1/\rho)/\mu$ gives
$$\sqrt{2G(\rho)}\ge\sqrt{2F_\infty}-\|\Sigma_1\|^{1/2}\|e_0\|\Big(\rho+\tfrac{\|\Sigma_1\|}{\mu(1-\rho)}\rho\log\tfrac1\rho\Big),$$
so $\liminf_{\rho\to0}G(\rho)\ge\sup_\tau F(\tau)\ge F_\infty$. The bound is on $\sqrt G$; squared, it reads $G\ge F_\infty-O(\sqrt{F_\infty}\,\rho\log(1/\rho))$.

*Proof.* Let $A=(1-\rho)\Sigma_2$ and $B=\rho\Sigma_1$. Duhamel's formula $e^{-(A+B)t}-e^{-At}=-\int_0^te^{-(A+B)(t-s)}Be^{-As}\,ds$, with $\|e^{-(A+B)s}\|,\|e^{-As}\|\le1$ for PSD exponents, gives $\|e^{-H_\rho t}e_0-e^{-At}e_0\|\le\rho t\|\Sigma_1\|\|e_0\|$. Since $e^{-At}e_0=e^{-\Sigma_2\tau}e_0$ with $\tau=(1-\rho)t$, the reverse triangle inequality applied to $\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}\cdot\|$ gives (4.1). For the second display, $\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}(e^{-\Sigma_2\tau}-P_{\ker\Sigma_2})e_0\|\le\|\Sigma_1\|^{1/2}e^{-\mu\tau}\|e_0\|$. $\square$

**Proposition 5 (a slow mode exists; an upper envelope).** If $F_\infty>0$, then $\lambda^+_{\min}(H_\rho)\le\rho\|\Sigma_1\|$. For all $t$, $L_1(t)\le\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1\|\|e_0\|^2e^{-2\lambda^+_{\min}(H_\rho)t}$. Moreover $\lambda^+_{\min}(H_\rho)\ge\min(\rho,1-\rho)\,\nu$ with $\nu=\lambda^+_{\min}(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2)$.

*Proof.* Let $z=P_{\ker\Sigma_2}e_0$. Then $\Sigma_1z\neq0$ because $F_\infty>0$. Split $z=z_K+z_R$ with $z_K\in\ker H_\rho\subseteq\ker\Sigma_2$. Then $z_R\in\ker\Sigma_2\cap\mathrm{range}\,H_\rho$ and $\Sigma_1z_R=\Sigma_1z\neq0$. The Rayleigh quotient on $\mathrm{range}\,H_\rho$ is $z_R^\top H_\rho z_R/\|z_R\|^2=\rho\,z_R^\top\Sigma_1z_R/\|z_R\|^2\le\rho\|\Sigma_1\|$. The envelope follows because $\Sigma_1^{1/2}P_{\ker H_\rho}=0$. For the last claim, $H_\rho\succeq\min(\rho,1-\rho)(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2)$ and the two matrices have the same kernel. $\square$

By itself Proposition 5 does *not* show that recovery is slow. It exhibits one slow eigenmode and bounds $L_1$ from above; nothing in it prevents that mode from carrying negligible old-task loss. The first submission drew the conclusion "recovery takes time of order $1/\rho$" from it, which was not justified. Theorem 7 below supplies the missing lower bound.

**Theorem 6 (paths that stay in $\ker\Sigma_1$ have no gap).** If $e(t)\in\ker\Sigma_1$ for all $t$, then $L_1(t)\equiv0$. In particular a straight segment from $e_0$ to any joint minimizer $e_\infty\in\ker\Sigma_1\cap\ker\Sigma_2$ has $L_1\equiv0$, because $\ker\Sigma_1$ is a subspace containing both endpoints. Two dynamics that produce such paths:

(a) *Exact Gauss–Newton/Newton on the replay objective*, $\dot w=-H_\rho^+\nabla L_\rho$, for any $\rho\in(0,1)$: $e(t)=e_0-(1-e^{-t})P_Re_0$ with $P_R$ the projector onto $\mathrm{range}\,H_\rho$, a straight line to $P_{\ker H_\rho}e_0$. The same holds for discrete steps $w\leftarrow w-\eta H_\rho^+\nabla L_\rho$ with $\eta\in(0,2)$.
(a′) *Projected gradient* (the OGD analogue of Farajtabar et al., 2020), $\dot w=-P_{\ker\Sigma_1}\nabla L_\rho$, for any $\rho\in[0,1)$ including $\rho=0$: $e(t)\in\ker\Sigma_1$ for all $t$, and $e(t)\to P_{\ker\Sigma_1\cap\ker\Sigma_2}e_0$, so $L_2\to0$ as well.
(b) *Damped Gauss–Newton*, $P=(H_\rho+\delta I)^{-1}$: $G\le\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1\|\|e_0\|^2\big(\delta/(e\,\lambda^+_{\min}(H_\rho))\big)^2$.
(c) For a general preconditioner $P\succ0$, $L_1(t)=\tfrac12(1-\rho)^2t^2\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}P\Sigma_2e_0\|^2+O(t^3)$.

*Proof.* (a) $H^+H=P_R$, so $e(t)=P_Ke_0+e^{-t}P_Re_0$ with $P_K=I-P_R$ (for discrete steps, $(1-\eta)^k$ in place of $e^{-t}$). $P_Ke_0\in\ker H_\rho\subseteq\ker\Sigma_1$ and $e_0\in\ker\Sigma_1$, so the whole segment is in $\ker\Sigma_1$. (a′) For $e\in\ker\Sigma_1$, $P_{\ker\Sigma_1}H_\rho e=(1-\rho)P_{\ker\Sigma_1}\Sigma_2e\in\ker\Sigma_1$, so the flow stays in $\ker\Sigma_1$ and is the gradient flow of $(1-\rho)L_2$ restricted to that subspace. It converges to the projection of $e_0$ onto $\{z\in\ker\Sigma_1:z^\top\Sigma_2z=0\}=\ker\Sigma_1\cap\ker\Sigma_2$. (b) $PH$ has the eigenvectors of $H$ with rates $\alpha_i=\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\delta)$. Comparing with (a) mode by mode, $|e^{-\alpha t}-e^{-t}|\le(1-\alpha)t\,e^{-\alpha t}\le(1-\alpha)/(\alpha e)=\delta/(\lambda e)$; modes in $\ker H$ do not move under either flow. (c) This is Theorem 2 with $H$ replaced by $PH$. $\square$

*What this theorem does and does not say.* Part (a) is the observation that the straight path between two zero-loss points of a PSD quadratic has zero loss, applied to a dynamics that happens to move in a straight line. It is the exact-model counterpart of the low-loss linear path that Kamath et al. (2024) found empirically. It does not single out the joint-curvature metric: (a′) achieves the same with no curvature information and no replay weight, using only the span of old inputs, and any other rule that moves within $\ker\Sigma_1$ (an exact projection onto the joint solution set, a min-norm solve) does too. What the theorem shows about GD is that its path in a non-commuting quadratic is curved and leaves $\ker\Sigma_1$. The Gauss–Newton route has one distinguishing property and one severe weakness. The property: it needs only the gradient and curvature of the replay objective itself, not a separate projector. The weakness: it is ill-conditioned in $\rho$. For every $\rho>0$ its endpoint has $L_1=0$, but at $\rho=0$ the Newton endpoint is $P_{\ker\Sigma_2}e_0$ with $L_1=F_\infty$; the cancellation relies on resolving eigenvalues of size $\lambda^+_{\min}(H_\rho)\le\rho\|\Sigma_1\|$ (Prop. 5), so by (b) a damping or curvature error of order $\rho\|\Sigma_1\|$ is enough to destroy it. This yields the prediction, tested and *not* confirmed in the network (§6), that the damping needed to suppress the gap scales like $\delta\propto\rho$.

**Theorem 7 (recovery time is $\Theta(1/\rho)$).** Assume $F_\infty>0$ and let $\beta=\sqrt{2F_\infty}\,/\,(\|\Sigma_1\|^{1/2}\|e_0\|)\in(0,1]$. Fix $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and put $c_\varepsilon=(1-\sqrt\varepsilon)\beta/2$.

(a) *Lower bound.* Let $t_b=c_\varepsilon/(\rho\|\Sigma_1\|)$. If $e^{-\mu(1-\rho)t_b}\le c_\varepsilon$, which holds for all sufficiently small $\rho$ (precisely, when $\rho/(1-\rho)\le\mu c_\varepsilon/(\|\Sigma_1\|\ln(1/c_\varepsilon))$), then $L_1(t_b)\ge\varepsilon F_\infty$ and therefore
$$T_\varepsilon(\rho)\;\ge\;\frac{(1-\sqrt\varepsilon)\sqrt{2F_\infty}}{2\rho\,\|\Sigma_1\|^{3/2}\|e_0\|}.$$
(b) *Upper bound.* For every $\rho\in(0,1)$,
$$T_\varepsilon(\rho)\;\le\;\frac{\ln\!\big(1/(\varepsilon\beta^2)\big)}{2\lambda^+_{\min}(H_\rho)}\;\le\;\frac{\ln\!\big(1/(\varepsilon\beta^2)\big)}{2\min(\rho,1-\rho)\,\nu}.$$

*Proof.* (a) Combine (4.1) with $\sqrt{2F(\tau)}\ge\sqrt{2F_\infty}-\|\Sigma_1\|^{1/2}e^{-\mu\tau}\|e_0\|$ (the last line of the proof of Theorem 4) to get $\sqrt{2L_1(t)}\ge\sqrt{2F_\infty}-\|\Sigma_1\|^{1/2}\|e_0\|\big(e^{-\mu(1-\rho)t}+\rho t\|\Sigma_1\|\big)$. At $t=t_b$ the bracket is at most $c_\varepsilon+c_\varepsilon=(1-\sqrt\varepsilon)\beta$, so $\sqrt{2L_1(t_b)}\ge\sqrt{2F_\infty}\,(1-(1-\sqrt\varepsilon))=\sqrt{2\varepsilon F_\infty}$. (b) By Proposition 5, $L_1(t)<\varepsilon F_\infty$ as soon as $\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1\|\|e_0\|^2e^{-2\lambda^+_{\min}t}<\varepsilon F_\infty$, that is $t>\ln(1/(\varepsilon\beta^2))/(2\lambda^+_{\min})$; then use the lower bound on $\lambda^+_{\min}$. $\square$

So for $\rho\le\min(\rho_0,\tfrac12)$ the old-task loss stays above $\varepsilon F_\infty$ for a time between $c_1/\rho$ and $c_2/\rho$, with $c_1,c_2$ depending only on the two tasks and $e_0$. The constants are far apart (§5, E5). The next proposition identifies the true constant.

**Proposition 8 (limiting constant).** Let $N=\ker\Sigma_2$, $M=P_N\Sigma_1P_N$ and $z(s)=e^{-Ms}P_Ne_0$, the gradient flow of the old-task loss *restricted to* $N$. Define $L^{\mathrm{slow}}(s)=\tfrac12 z(s)^\top\Sigma_1z(s)$ and $T^\star_\varepsilon=\sup\{s:L^{\mathrm{slow}}(s)\ge\varepsilon F_\infty\}$. Then $L^{\mathrm{slow}}(0)=F_\infty$, $L^{\mathrm{slow}}$ is strictly decreasing while positive, and $\rho\,T_\varepsilon(\rho)\to T^\star_\varepsilon$ as $\rho\to0$.

*Proof (outline).* On the slow time scale $s=\rho t$, $e(s/\rho)=\exp(-s(\Sigma_1+\kappa\Sigma_2))e_0$ with $\kappa=(1-\rho)/\rho\to\infty$. In the decomposition $\mathbb R^d=N\oplus N^\perp$ the matrix $\Sigma_1+\kappa\Sigma_2$ has blocks $M$, $C$, $C^\top$ and $D+\kappa S$ with $S\succeq\mu I$ on $N^\perp$. By the Schur complement, $\dim N$ of its eigenvalues converge to those of $M$ with eigenvectors converging into $N$, and the others are at least $\kappa\mu\to\infty$ (Courant–Fischer on $N^\perp$). Hence $\exp(-s(\Sigma_1+\kappa\Sigma_2))\to P_Ne^{-sM}P_N$ uniformly on $s\in[s_0,\infty)$ for every $s_0>0$, and $L_1(s/\rho)\to L^{\mathrm{slow}}(s)$ uniformly there. Since $z(s)\in N$, $L^{\mathrm{slow}}(s)=\tfrac12z^\top Mz$ and $\tfrac{d}{ds}L^{\mathrm{slow}}=-\|Mz\|^2<0$ while $L^{\mathrm{slow}}>0$, so the level $\varepsilon F_\infty$ is crossed once and transversally; uniform convergence then gives convergence of the last exit time. $\square$

We give this argument in outline only (the eigenvalue-convergence step is the standard large-coupling limit) and verify the conclusion numerically in E5. Read together, Theorems 2, 4 and 7 and Proposition 8 describe replay at small $\rho$: the fall begins at nearly the same rate as without replay, it is almost as deep, and the time to recover is $T^\star_\varepsilon/\rho$. Replay does not remove the new task's pull on the old solution; it adds a restoring flow, of speed proportional to $\rho$, inside the set of new-task solutions.

**Theorem 9 (mini-batch SGD with replay).** Let each step draw $B_1=\rho B$ old and $B_2=(1-\rho)B$ new samples uniformly with replacement (with $\rho B$ an integer) and update $e_{k+1}=(I-\eta\hat H_k)e_k$, $\hat H_k=\tfrac1B\sum_{b}x_bx_b^\top$. Let $S_k=\mathbb E[e_ke_k^\top]$ and $\mathcal Q_j(S)=\mathbb E_{x\sim\text{task }j}[xx^\top Sxx^\top]$. Then

(a) $S_{k+1}=(I-\eta H_\rho)S_k(I-\eta H_\rho)+\dfrac{\eta^2}{B^2}\sum_{j=1,2}B_j\big(\mathcal Q_j(S_k)-\Sigma_jS_k\Sigma_j\big)$, and $\mathbb E[L_1(w_k)]=\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_1S_k)$.
(b) $S_k\succeq S_k^{\mathrm{GD}}$ for all $k$, where $S^{\mathrm{GD}}_k=e_ke_k^\top$ is the full-batch GD iterate with the same $\eta$. Hence $\mathbb E[L_1^{\mathrm{SGD}}(k)]\ge L_1^{\mathrm{GD}}(k)$ for every $k$, and $\mathbb E[\max_kL_1^{\mathrm{SGD}}(k)]\ge G^{\mathrm{GD}}$: at a fixed step size, sampling noise cannot shrink the expected gap.
(c) First step: with $a=\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2e_0\|^2$ and $b=\mathbb E_{x\sim2}[(x^\top e_0)^2\,x^\top\Sigma_1x]\ge a$,
$$\mathbb E[L_1(w_1)]=\tfrac12\eta^2(1-\rho)\Big[(1-\rho)\,a+\frac{b-a}{B}\Big].$$

*Proof.* (a) Realizability makes every per-sample residual $x^\top e$, so the update is linear in $e$ with i.i.d. random matrices. $\mathbb E[\hat H]=H_\rho$, and $\mathbb E[\hat HS\hat H]=H_\rho SH_\rho+\tfrac1{B^2}\sum_b\big(\mathbb E[A_bSA_b]-\mathbb E[A_b]S\,\mathbb E[A_b]\big)$ with $A_b=x_bx_b^\top$, because distinct draws are independent. (b) Each noise term is $\mathbb E[(A-\mathbb EA)S(A-\mathbb EA)]\succeq0$ for $S\succeq0$ and is monotone in $S$; so is $S\mapsto(I-\eta H)S(I-\eta H)$. Induction from $S_0=S_0^{\mathrm{GD}}$ gives the order, and $\mathbb E\max\ge\max\mathbb E$. (c) Put $S_0=e_0e_0^\top$. Because $X_1e_0=0$, $\mathcal Q_1(S_0)=0$ and $\Sigma_1S_0\Sigma_1=0$: old samples contribute no noise at the first step. The mean term gives $\tfrac12\eta^2(1-\rho)^2a$ as in Theorem 2, and the new-sample noise gives $\tfrac12\eta^2\tfrac{B_2}{B^2}(b-a)$ with $B_2/B^2=(1-\rho)/B$. $b\ge a$ is the PSD statement of (b) at $S_0$. $\square$

So replay changes the $(1-\rho)^2$ law under SGD: the noise part of the first step is reduced by replay only linearly, by $(1-\rho)$, because replay removes new samples from the batch without the old samples being able to oppose anything yet.

**Proposition 10 (finite replay buffer).** Let the buffer hold $m\le n_1$ of the old samples, with second-moment matrix $\Sigma_M$, and train by gradient flow on $(1-\rho)L_2+\rho L_M$. Then for every $\rho\in(0,1)$: (a) $L_1(\infty)=\tfrac12\|\Sigma_1^{1/2}P_{\ker\Sigma_M\cap\ker\Sigma_2}e_0\|^2$, independent of $\rho$; (b) the first-step law of Theorem 2 holds unchanged; (c) inequality (4.1) holds with $\|\Sigma_1\|^{3/2}$ replaced by $\|\Sigma_1\|^{1/2}\|\Sigma_M\|$.

*Proof.* $\ker\Sigma_1\subseteq\ker\Sigma_M$, so $\Sigma_Me_0=0$. (a) is Proposition 1 applied to $(\Sigma_M,\Sigma_2)$, evaluated with $\Sigma_1$. (b) and (c) repeat the proofs of Theorems 2 and 4 with $B=\rho\Sigma_M$. $\square$

With a partial buffer the replay weight controls only the transient and the speed; the terminal forgetting is fixed by which samples are in the buffer. This endpoint is the object studied by Mahdaviyeh et al. (2025), who show it need not decrease with $m$.

## 6 Nonlinear Test: Two-Layer Networks on Real Data

**Design.** We ask which of the linear claims survive in a nonlinear network trained on real data. The model is a 64–16–10 tanh MLP (1,210 parameters) trained on the sklearn `digits` dataset (1,797 8×8 images; 1,200 for training, 597 for testing). We use squared loss on one-hot targets, which keeps the Gauss–Newton matrix $J^\top J$ the exact analogue of $\Sigma_k$ (Hui & Belkin, 2021, show that square loss is competitive for classification). We use two scenarios (van de Ven et al., 2022):

- **perm** (domain-incremental): task 2 applies a fixed random pixel permutation to a disjoint half of the training images. The 10-way head is shared.
- **split** (class-incremental): task 1 is classes 0–4 and task 2 is classes 5–9, with a shared 10-way head.

Phase 1 runs 4,000 full-batch GD steps on task 1. Phase 2 optimizes $(1-\rho)L_2+\rho L_1$ with the *entire* task-1 training set replayed. This is incremental joint training, the most favourable replay regime and the one in which Kamath et al. (2024) observed the gap. Throughout §6 the gap depth $G=\max_tL_1(t)-L_1(0)$ is measured on the old task's **training** loss, and every accuracy is measured on the old task's **test** set. The main sweep (`code/mlp_experiments.py`, `results/mlp_summary.json`) uses 4 seeds × 7 values of $\rho$ and compares GD with $\eta=1/\lambda_{\max}(G_\rho(\theta_0))$ for 4,000 steps, Adam (lr $10^{-3}$) for 4,000 steps, and damped Gauss–Newton (GGN) for 60–150 steps; "± sd" there is the population standard deviation over the 4 seeds. Paired statistics for that sweep are in `results/mlp_audit_r1.json` (`code/audit_mlp_r1.py`). The matched comparison, the common-step short-time test and the linearization test (`code/mlp_matched.py`, `results/mlpm_summary.json`) use 6 seeds. Confidence intervals are two-sided 95% $t$-intervals over seeds; seeds are shared across methods, so differences are paired. Figure 3 (`results/fig3_mlp_curves.png`) shows the old-task loss and accuracy trajectories and Figure 4 (`results/fig4_mlp_scaling.png`) the scaling and optimizer comparisons.

**A stability gap appears under full joint replay.** In split with GD at $\rho=0.1$, old-task test accuracy falls from 99.3% to a minimum of 46.3±6.0% and recovers to 87.5±2.2% by step 4,000. At $\rho=0.3$ the corresponding values are 99.3% → 93.6% → 95.5%. In perm with GD at $\rho=0.1$, accuracy goes 94.5% → 78.1% → 84.8%. Unlike the realizable linear model, recovery here is incomplete within the budget, so $G$ includes a terminal part. For split at $\rho=0.1$, the transient part is $G-(L_1(T)-L_1(0))=0.377-0.141=0.236$. The network is in the regime of Proposition 10 and E4 (a transient on top of residual forgetting), not the clean realizable regime.

**Prediction 1 (first-step law, Thm 2): holds at step one, where it says little; holds over several steps only for small $\rho$.** Across all 56 GD runs of the main sweep, the ratio of the measured first-step increase of $L_1$ to $\tfrac12\eta^2(1-\rho)^2g_2^\top G_1g_2$ is 0.994 on average (range 0.979–1.000). As the first-round review pointed out, this is close to a tautology: for one small step from a minimum of $L_1$, a second-order Taylor expansion with the measured Gauss–Newton matrix is nearly exact whatever the theory says. We therefore no longer count it as evidence for the theory beyond its first-order content. The informative question is how long the $\rho$-dependence stays a pure $(1-\rho)^2$ prefactor. We run GD from $\theta_0$ for 30 steps with a *common* step $\eta_c=1/\max(\lambda_{\max}(G_1),\lambda_{\max}(G_2))$ for $\rho\in\{0,0.01,0.1,0.3,0.5\}$ and form $R_\rho(k)=[\Delta L_1(k)/(1-\rho)^2]/\Delta L_1(k)|_{\rho=0}$, which equals 1 if replay acts only through the prefactor (6 seeds; `short` in `mlpm_summary.json`; Figure 7, `results/fig7_mlp_short_lin.png`). At $k=1$, $R_\rho$ is within 0.6% of 1 for every $\rho$. At $\rho=0.01$ it is still 0.98–0.99 at $k=10$ and 0.96–0.97 at $k=30$. At $\rho=0.1$ it has fallen to 0.86 (perm; CI 0.83–0.88) and 0.89 (split; 0.88–0.90) by $k=10$ and to 0.71 and 0.78 by $k=30$; at $\rho=0.3$ it is 0.72 and 0.76 at $k=10$ and 0.55 and 0.60 at $k=30$. So the $(1-\rho)^2$ law describes the first step, and the first tens of steps only when $\rho\le0.01$. For moderate $\rho$ replay begins to oppose the drift within the first few steps, which is what the linear model also predicts beyond leading order.

**Prediction 2 (small replay leaves most of the gap, Thm 4): confirmed qualitatively.** $G(\rho)/G(0)$ for GD is 0.76 / 0.55 / 0.37 / 0.24 / 0.11 / 0.06 in perm and 0.89 / 0.74 / 0.58 / 0.39 / 0.18 / 0.09 in split, for $\rho=0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5$ (Figure 4a). This is close to the linear model's 0.88 / 0.77 / 0.60 / 0.38 / 0.19 / 0.10. In split with $\rho\le0.01$, old-task accuracy reaches 0% at its minimum, exactly as without replay.

**Prediction 3 (recovery time $\propto1/\rho$, Thm 7): not testable in this network; tested in an over-parameterized one.** In the main sweep with $\rho\le0.03$, half-recovery from the peak was not reached within 4,000 steps in any run (all 4 seeds, both scenarios). In split it was reached in every run at $\rho\ge0.1$, at steps 484–2,445 ($\rho=0.1$), 244–1,189 ($\rho=0.3$) and 804–1,770 ($\rho=0.5$) across individual seeds; in perm 6 of the 12 runs at $\rho\ge0.1$ were still censored. (The first submission quoted "600–1,600", which was the range of seed *means*.) These runs are censored and have nonzero terminal forgetting, so they cannot test a scaling law. We did not obtain a verified measurement of recovery-time scaling in any network: an experiment in a larger, over-parameterized network was started but not completed or checked, and we report nothing from it. Theorem 7 is therefore untested outside the linear model.

**How far does the linearization at the task switch predict the network?** The linear theory applies exactly to the network linearized at $\theta_0$, $f\approx f_0+J(\theta-\theta_0)$, whose task Hessians are the Gauss–Newton matrices at the switch. We run GD on that linearized model with the same step as the real GD run and compare (6 seeds; `lin` in `mlpm_summary.json`; Figure 7, right panel). So in this network the *fall* into the gap is, to a first approximation, the linear phenomenon of §4 with the network's own tangent kernel; the *recovery* is not, which is consistent with the failure of the predictions that depend on the slow modes (damping scaling, exact elimination).

**Summary of transfer.** In the network: (i) the insensitivity of gap depth to small $\rho$ holds qualitatively, with numbers close to the linear model's; (ii) the depth of the gap is predicted to within about 10% by the linearization at the task switch; (iii) the $(1-\rho)^2$ law holds at step one and for tens of steps only at $\rho\le0.01$; (iv) a flow-like curvature-aware update reduces the gap by 34–75% at matched new-task progress for $\rho\in\{0.1,0.3\}$, and this is not a step-size effect; (v) exact elimination, the full-step guarantee, the benefit at $\rho=0.01$ and the damping-scaling prediction do not transfer; (vi) recovery-time scaling and the existence criterion were not tested.

## 7 Discussion

**What the theory explains, and where the explanation stops.** Several features of the stability gap have been reported empirically: it occurs under full joint training (Kamath et al., 2024), modest replay does not remove it (De Lange et al., 2023; Harun & Kanan, 2024), and optimizer-level changes reduce it (Yoo et al., 2024; Rodriguez-Garcia et al., 2025). In the linear model these follow from one structural fact. When the curvatures of the two tasks are not aligned (Theorem 3), the replay objective has a fast new-task mode and slow old-task modes of rate $O(\rho)$. Gradient flow follows the fast mode first and the slow, replay-driven correction later. The depth of the trough is set by the ratio of time scales (Theorems 1 and 4) and its duration by $1/\rho$ (Theorem 7), not by whether the optimum is shared. This is an explanation *in the model*. In our network only the depth insensitivity transferred cleanly.

We withdraw one claim of the first submission. We wrote that the head localization of Łapacz et al. (2025) "fits this picture" because a shared linear head trained on new classes "is exactly a linear least-squares problem of the kind analysed here". That is not so: their heads are trained with cross-entropy, the head problem is generally not realizable, and the features under the head move during training. Our model is at most suggestive for that finding, and we make no claim about it.

**Why "more replay" is a weak lever against the transient.** Theorems 4 and 7 show that a small replay weight purchases eventual recovery, at a time cost $T^\star_\varepsilon/\rho$, and buys almost no protection during the transition. In both the linear model and the MLP, $\rho=0.01$ leaves 55–94% of the no-replay damage at its peak (94% in the 2-D model, 77% in the $d=100$ model, 55–74% in the MLP). E7 adds a qualification: this is a statement about the good-replay regime. With a small buffer the dominant problem is terminal forgetting, and the transient excess is minor.

**What Theorem 6 does and does not license.** In the first submission we called the Gauss–Newton result the sharpest statement in the paper and concluded that the stability gap "is an artefact of Euclidean gradient geometry". Both were overstatements. The theorem says that GD's path in a non-commuting quadratic is curved and leaves the old task's null space, while paths that stay in it, of which the Gauss–Newton path is one and the projected-gradient path another, have no gap. It does not show that the joint-curvature metric is privileged, and the Gauss–Newton route is ill-conditioned exactly where replay is small. In the network, flow-like GGN steps did reduce the gap at $\rho\in\{0.1,0.3\}$, but full steps did not, small $\rho$ did not, and reducing the damping helped in one scenario of two. The supportable statement is narrow: in the linear model the gap is a property of the path GD takes, not of the objective, and it can be avoided by several rules that all need accurate knowledge of the old task's input span or of curvature at scale $\rho$.

**Predictions for larger models, with their status.** The first submission listed five "pre-registered predictions". Given that the damping prediction already failed at our own scale, we restate them with the evidence each one has. Setting: continual pre-training or fine-tuning with a replay mixture weight $\rho$ (e.g. Gupta et al., 2023), with the old-domain loss logged every step.
- **P1 (first-step law).** Immediately after a switch, the old-domain loss increase over the first step, divided by $(1-\rho)^2$, is independent of $\rho$ for full-batch updates; for mini-batches of size $B$ there is an additional term proportional to $(1-\rho)/B$ (Theorem 9c). *Status:* a Taylor-expansion statement, expected to hold; our network data show the collapse is lost within a few steps at $\rho\ge0.1$. It is a weak test of the theory.
- **P2 (depth insensitivity).** For $\rho\le0.05$ the peak old-loss increase is at least 50% of the no-replay increase. *Status:* supported in the linear model and in both MLP scenarios (0.55 and 0.74 at $\rho=0.01$; 0.37 and 0.58 at $\rho=0.03$, so perm at $\rho=0.03$ is already below the 50% line and the threshold $0.05$ is too generous for that scenario). We lower the claim to $\rho\le0.01$.
- **P3 (recovery scaling).** With enough capacity for the joint problem, the time to return within a fixed fraction of the no-replay damage scales as $\rho^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha\to1$ as $\rho\to0$. *Status:* a theorem in the linear model; **untested** in any network (our 1,210-parameter runs are censored, and we have no verified larger run).
- **P4 (geometry).** At matched new-domain loss, an optimizer whose curvature statistics include the replay data shows a smaller gap than SGD/AdamW. *Status:* supported at our scale for an *exact* damped Gauss–Newton step with small step size at $\rho\in\{0.1,0.3\}$ (Table 7), not at $\rho=0.01$, and not for full steps. We drop the sub-claim about damping scaling with $\rho$, which failed in our network, and we have no evidence about K-FAC-type approximations.
- **P5 (existence).** Tasks whose gradient covariances are nearly co-diagonalizable show smaller gaps. *Status:* a theorem in the linear model; **untested** in any network, including ours. The first-round review asked for this test at our scale and we did not run it.

## 8 Limitations

- **The model is deliberately minimal.** The theorems are for linear regression with exact realizability and two tasks. Theorem 9 covers mini-batch SGD only through its second moments, at a fixed step size, with sampling with replacement and a fixed batch composition; it gives no result on the distribution of the per-run maximum beyond $\mathbb E\max\ge\max\mathbb E$. We do not analyse momentum, learning-rate schedules, or more than two tasks.
- **Novelty is incremental.** The mathematics is elementary and the conceptual messages restate known empirical findings in a solvable model (§1). There is no result for feature learning: the comparison with the linearized network in §6 is an experiment, not a proof that the predictions survive to first order.
- **Proposition 8 is proved in outline.** We rely on the standard large-coupling limit for the eigenvalue step and on numerical verification ($\rho T_{0.1}$ within $8\times10^{-5}$ of $T^\star_{0.1}$ at $\rho=10^{-5}$ on 10 instances).
- **The bounds are loose.** The Theorem 7 bounds are off by factors of about 350 (lower) and 200 (upper) on our instances, and the lower bound's sufficient condition only holds for $\rho\lesssim3\times10^{-5}$ there. The Theorem 4 lower bound and the Theorem 6(b) upper bound hold on every instance tested but are not tight. The closed form (Theorem 1) is exact only for rank-one tasks.
- **The SGD experiment uses one small step size.** At $\eta\lambda_{\max}\approx0.03$ the noise raised the expected peak by at most 2.7%. We did not study large steps, where the effect could be much larger.
- **The nonlinear test is small.** One architecture with 1,210 parameters, one dataset (sklearn digits), squared loss, full-batch training, the entire old set replayed. This is far from the regime where the stability gap matters in practice (SGD, finite buffers, cross-entropy, deep networks). The cross-entropy GGN attempt we tried first diverged under saturated softmax outputs, and we switched to squared loss for that reason.
- **Statistics.** The main sweep has 4 seeds and the damping test 2; the matched comparison has 6 (8 were planned; two were lost to a machine restart and not re-run). Intervals are $t$-intervals over seeds with no correction for the number of comparisons reported. With 4 seeds a paired $t$-interval is fragile.
- **The damping-scaling prediction failed in the network** (Table 8), and we have not identified why. One untested possibility is that the Gauss–Newton matrix changes along the trajectory enough that the invariance argument of Theorem 6(a) holds only locally.
- **The GGN comparison is not compute-matched.** GGN steps cost $O(P^3)$ here. Our claim concerns the trajectory, not wall-clock efficiency. Practical use would require K-FAC-style approximations, whose effect on the gap we did not measure.
- **Not done.** The commutator test of P5 in the network; an NTK-level proof; more than two tasks. A recovery-time measurement in a network. A partial exploration of momentum, other first-order methods and network recovery times was started and not finished; it is in `code/exploratory_unfinished/` and nothing in this paper depends on it.
- **Prior work.** To our knowledge, no prior work gives a closed-form or provable characterization of the stability gap in linear models. Our searches (November 2026) found only empirical and conceptual treatments, but we cannot exclude concurrent work.

## 9 Conclusion

In the simplest setting where the question can be posed exactly, overparameterized linear regression with replay, replay removes terminal forgetting completely, yet the path forgets almost as much as training without replay, and for a time proportional to $1/\rho$. The gap exists exactly when the new task's curvature does not preserve the old task's null space, and it is absent for any update rule whose path stays in that null space. Mini-batch noise does not reduce it in expectation, and a finite buffer adds a $\rho$-independent terminal floor beneath it. In a small network on real data, the insensitivity of the gap depth to small replay weights reappears; the fall into the gap is predicted to within about 10% by the network's linearization at the task switch, the $(1-\rho)^2$ law holds at the first step but only briefly beyond it, and a flow-like Gauss–Newton update reduces the gap by 34–75% at matched progress for moderate replay weights. Exact elimination does not survive, and the damping prediction fails. The theory gives the stability gap a precise vocabulary and a set of quantities that can be computed in a model; whether those quantities govern the gap in networks of practical size is not established here.
