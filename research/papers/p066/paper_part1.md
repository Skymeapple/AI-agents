# Offloading Traps in Adolescent Skill Formation: A Calibrated Dynamic Model of Generative-AI Help, Present Bias, and Developmental Plasticity

**Target venue:** AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES 2027), full paper track

---

## Abstract

Randomized evidence now shows that unrestricted generative-AI help can raise adolescents' assisted performance while lowering their unassisted performance (Bastani et al., 2025), and that brief AI assistance reduces persistence on subsequent unaided problems (Liu et al., 2026). What these short experiments cannot tell us is how such effects compound over the years of adolescence, who is most exposed, and what properties a "guardrail" must have to prevent harm. We build and analyze a dynamic model of skill formation in which a present-biased learner decides, each practice session, how much cognitive work to hand to an AI assistant. Skill grows only through the learner's own effortful work (with a self-productivity term in the spirit of Cunha and Heckman, 2007) and decays otherwise; a second state, persistence, is built by successful unaided work and eroded by receiving complete answers. We prove that, when the assistant yields no learning, the learner's offloading rule has a threshold form and the skill dynamics are bistable: learners who start below a computable threshold s_L converge to an absorbing low-skill state (an "offloading trap"), while those above it converge to the no-AI steady state. The threshold rises with assistant quality and effort cost and falls with present-bias parameter beta and learning plasticity eta; in the reduced model it depends on beta and eta only through their product, which yields a testable prediction about the age profile of vulnerability. Any positive pedagogical retention sigma (learning retained when the AI does the work, as with hint-giving tutors) removes the absorbing state, but the long-run skill under perpetual tutor use is s_sigma < s*, strictly increasing in sigma. We then calibrate the two-state model to the six arm-level means of the Bastani et al. field experiment (nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school students) by searching for all parameter vectors that reproduce them within tolerance. The data pin down the assistants' in-the-moment quality and require a high retention for the guardrailed tutor (sigma about 0.9 in the identified set), attribute most of the 17% exam penalty to the skill channel rather than the persistence channel, and leave the learner's present bias and effort cost unidentified, because nearly all students in both AI arms offload almost completely. Projections across the identified set and a sweep of untestable long-run assumptions show that a large long-run deficit under unguarded AI is robust, that hint-style tutors bound the loss, and that whether a guardrailed tutor helps depends on whether an unguarded assistant of higher in-the-moment quality is also available. We state five falsifiable predictions and a pre-registered test protocol. All results come from code we ran; no new human data were collected.

## 中文摘要

随机对照研究已经表明：不加限制的生成式AI帮助可以提高青少年"有AI时"的练习成绩，却降低其"无AI时"的独立成绩（Bastani 等，2025），而且短时间的AI辅助会降低随后独立解题时的坚持性（Liu 等，2026）。但这些短期实验无法回答：上述效应在整个青春期如何累积？谁最易受影响？一个"护栏"必须具备什么性质才能避免伤害？本文构建并分析了一个技能形成的动态模型：一名具有现时偏好（present bias）的学习者在每次练习中决定把多少认知工作交给AI助手。技能只能通过学习者自身的努力增长（含Cunha与Heckman式的"自我生产性"项），否则会衰减；第二个状态变量"坚持性"由成功的独立练习建立，由直接获得完整答案而被侵蚀。我们证明：当AI帮助不带来任何学习时，学习者的外包决策具有阈值形式，技能动态呈双稳态——起点低于可计算阈值 s_L 的学习者收敛到一个吸收性的低技能状态（"外包陷阱"），高于阈值者收敛到无AI时的稳态。该阈值随AI质量和努力成本上升，随现时偏好参数 beta 和学习可塑性 eta 下降；在简化模型中阈值只通过乘积 beta·eta 依赖这两个参数，由此得到关于易感性年龄曲线的可检验预测。只要"教学保留率" sigma（AI代劳时仍保留的学习比例，如只给提示的导师式AI）为正，吸收态即消失，但长期使用导师式AI的技能 s_sigma 仍低于无AI稳态 s*，且随 sigma 严格递增。随后，我们以 Bastani 等人现场实验（近1000名土耳其高中生）报告的六个组别均值为目标，搜索所有能在容差内复现这些数据的参数向量（识别集）。数据精确确定了两种AI在练习中的即时质量，要求带护栏导师具有很高的保留率（识别集中 sigma 约为0.9），并把17%的考试损失主要归因于技能渠道而非坚持性渠道；而学习者的现时偏好与努力成本无法识别，因为两个AI组中几乎所有学生都几乎完全外包。在识别集和一系列无法检验的长期假设下的预测表明：无护栏AI导致的大幅长期缺陷是稳健的；提示型导师可以限制损失；而带护栏导师是否有效，取决于是否同时存在即时质量更高的无护栏助手。文末给出五条可证伪预测和一个预注册检验方案。所有数值结果均来自我们实际运行的代码，未收集新的人类数据。

## 1 Introduction

Generative AI has entered adolescents' schoolwork quickly. In a Pew Research Center survey of U.S. teens fielded in autumn 2024, 26% of 13- to 17-year-olds said they had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, double the 2023 share (Pew Research Center, 2025). A nationally representative Common Sense Media survey of 1,060 U.S. teens in spring 2025 found that 72% had used AI companions at least once and 52% used them at least a few times a month (Common Sense Media, 2025). The American Psychological Association issued a health advisory on AI and adolescent well-being in June 2025 that, among other things, called for more rigorous research on effects on youth (APA, 2025).

The best causal evidence on cognition comes from short experiments. In a field RCT with nearly 1,000 high-school students in Turkey, access to an unrestricted GPT-4 interface ("GPT Base") raised scores on assisted practice problems by 48% but lowered scores on a subsequent unassisted exam by 17%. A version prompted to give teacher-designed hints rather than answers ("GPT Tutor") raised practice scores by 127% and left exam scores statistically indistinguishable from control (Bastani et al., 2025). Across three online experiments (N = 1,222), roughly 10 to 15 minutes of AI assistance reduced subsequent unassisted performance and increased the rate at which participants skipped problems (Liu et al., 2026). Survey and lab studies report related associations: lower self-reported critical-thinking effort with higher confidence in AI (Lee et al., 2025), lower critical-thinking scores among heavier and younger AI users (Gerlich, 2025), and less metacognitive activity with ChatGPT support despite better essay scores (Fan et al., 2025). On the other side, a carefully designed AI tutor outperformed in-class active learning in a university physics RCT (Kestin et al., 2025). Design matters.

These studies leave three questions open, and they are the questions that matter for adolescents in particular:

1. **Compounding.** A one-semester exam penalty is small. Does it shrink, persist, or grow when the same choice is made weekly for six years while the learner's skill and disposition change in response?
2. **Who is exposed.** Adolescents discount the future more steeply than adults (Green et al., 1994; Steinberg et al., 2009) and may also learn faster during a period of high plasticity (Fuhrmann et al., 2015, who also note that direct evidence for adolescent sensitive periods is limited). These two forces push in opposite directions. Which dominates?
3. **What a guardrail must do.** "Give hints, not answers" works in one trial. What property of a tool is doing the work, how much of it is needed, and does it survive when students can switch to an unguarded tool?

Short RCTs cannot answer these directly, and multi-year experiments that randomize adolescents' AI access are slow and raise ethical issues. A model that is (i) simple enough to analyze, (ii) disciplined by the existing experimental moments, and (iii) explicit about what the data do and do not pin down can say which long-run conclusions are robust, which hinge on untested assumptions, and which measurements would discriminate between them.

**Contributions.**

1. **A developmental model of AI offloading** (Section 3) with two state variables, skill and persistence. A present-biased learner chooses each session how much of the work to delegate to an assistant characterized by two numbers: in-the-moment quality q and pedagogical retention sigma, the share of learning preserved when the assistant does the work.
2. **Analytic results for the reduced model** (Section 4): a threshold offloading rule; bistability with an absorbing low-skill "offloading trap" when sigma = 0; signed comparative statics of the threshold in q, effort cost, present bias beta and plasticity eta; a product-invariance result (the threshold depends on beta and eta only through beta·eta); and a characterization of hint-style tutors (any sigma > 0 removes the absorbing state, and long-run skill under perpetual tutor use is s_sigma, strictly increasing in sigma). We verify each result numerically and report where it breaks down in a full dynamic-programming version.
3. **A calibration to field-experiment data** (Section 5). We search for every parameter vector that reproduces the six arm-level means of Bastani et al. (2025) within tolerance and report the identified set rather than a point estimate. The data tightly identify assistant quality, require high retention for the guardrailed tutor, attribute most of the exam penalty to skill rather than persistence, and do not identify present bias or effort cost.
4. **Long-run projections with explicit sensitivity analysis** (Section 6) across the identified set and a grid of untestable long-run assumptions: time-scale transfer, plasticity decay, maturation of beta, and modern assistant quality. We also run an exposure-timing experiment and a global rank-correlation sensitivity analysis.
5. **Five falsifiable predictions and a test protocol** (Section 7), including one that can be checked on the public replication data of Bastani et al. (2025).

We do not claim that AI harms adolescent cognition in general. The model shows under which conditions and through which mechanism harm would compound, which design property prevents it, and what evidence is still missing.

## 2 Related Work

**Cognitive offloading.** Risko and Gilbert (2016) define cognitive offloading as using physical action to change the information-processing demands of a task, and they propose a metacognitive account in which people offload when they believe it is more efficient than relying on internal ability. Those beliefs are not always accurate. Sparrow et al. (2011) showed that expecting future access to information lowers recall of the information itself while improving recall of where it is stored. Bjork's (1994) "desirable difficulties" explain why practice conditions that speed acquisition can impair long-term retention. Generative AI pushes offloading to its limit, because it can do the whole task rather than a memory sub-step.

**Evidence on generative AI and learning.** Bastani et al. (2025) is the central causal study for adolescents and supplies our calibration targets. Liu et al. (2026) provide causal evidence for a persistence channel; our second state variable is designed to represent it. Kosmyna et al. (2025) report lower EEG connectivity and weaker recall of one's own essay among adults writing with an LLM, but the study is small (54 participants; 18 in the crossover session) and has drawn methodological criticism (Stankovic et al., 2026). We therefore use it only as motivation. Fan et al. (2025) report "metacognitive laziness". Lee et al. (2025) and Gerlich (2025) report survey associations. Kestin et al. (2025) show that a pedagogically designed AI tutor can beat active learning. Our sigma parameter is a compact way to describe what separates these designs.

**Formal models of AI-induced deskilling.** Three recent working papers are closest to ours. Caosun and Aral (2026) study a worker whose AI use raises output immediately while skill adjusts slowly. They show that steady-state skill loss can occur under full foresight and that managerial short-termism can produce an "augmentation trap" and divergence between high- and low-skill workers. Aouad, Lykouris and Zhong (2026) model utility-maximizing effort with AI assistance as a substitute for effort, derive productivity paradoxes, and show skill polarization in steady state. Steyvers and Mayer (2025) treat the AI's decision of when to help as a POMDP over latent user engagement. Our work differs in four ways. (i) The decision-maker is an adolescent with quasi-hyperbolic present bias and age-varying plasticity, not a worker or manager, and the product-invariance and timing results are specific to that developmental framing. (ii) We separate the assistant into quality q and pedagogical retention sigma and derive what sigma does to the trap. (iii) We include a persistence state motivated by Liu et al. (2026) and Bandura's (1977) account of mastery experiences. (iv) We calibrate to adolescent field-experiment moments and report an identified set, including which parameters the data cannot pin down.

**Present bias and skill formation.** We use the quasi-hyperbolic (beta-delta) model (Laibson, 1997) with naive agents (O'Donoghue and Rabin, 1999). Present bias is stronger for effort than for money (Augenblick et al., 2015), and discounting declines from childhood through adulthood (Green et al., 1994; Steinberg et al., 2009). The self-productivity of skill, where skill raises the productivity of further investment, comes from Cunha and Heckman (2007), and the scaffolding idea from Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976).

**Adolescent well-being and AI.** The APA advisory (2025) and the Common Sense Media survey (2025) document broad adoption of AI and companion use. We address the cognitive-development side of the question (skill and academic persistence). Socio-emotional effects of companion chatbots are outside our model and are discussed only as an extension.
