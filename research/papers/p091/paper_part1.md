# Memorised Mistakes or Mistaken Audits? Separating Audit Error, Plausibility and Memorisation in Wrong-Key Agreement on MMLU

**Target venue:** NeurIPS 2027, Datasets & Benchmarks Track

## Abstract

Benchmarks are often called "saturated" or "contaminated" from leaderboard scores alone. We study one signal that is often proposed for telling the two apart: *wrong-key agreement*, meaning how often a model picks a benchmark's original answer key on items that an audit has flagged as wrongly keyed. A model that memorised the test set should reproduce the wrong keys. A model that reasons should not. We formalise this test as a latent-class response model with three mechanisms: (i) the wrong key being plausible, (ii) errors in the audit itself, and (iii) memorisation. We prove two things. First, memorisation concentrated on particular items cannot be identified from response data alone, because it is observationally equivalent to audit error. Second, the naive test (a positive association between model ability and agreement with flagged keys) reports memorisation whenever the audit-error rate exceeds a threshold η\* that can be computed from the data. We apply the framework to 80 models from the public HELM MMLU leaderboard, scored item by item against the MMLU-Redux annotations. The naive test is significant (Spearman ρ = 0.37, p < 0.001 across the 80 models). It looks like memorisation that grows with capability. The mixture model attributes this pattern to errors in the audit. An estimated 14.7% of the 101 usable "wrong ground truth" flags (≈15 items) behave like correctly keyed items. On the remaining items, agreement with the flagged key *falls* with ability (ρ = −0.53). We re-checked the 23 most suspicious items by hand: for 10 the original key is defensible and 8 are ambiguous. The likelihood-ratio test for memorisation that is homogeneous across items is null (LR = 0; bootstrap p = 1.0). The profile likelihood bounds such memorisation below roughly 0.1–0.2% of responses, and no individual model is flagged after FDR control. The same data show that MMLU is saturated as a measuring instrument rather than contaminated. We estimate a label-noise ceiling and report how close the best HELM models are to it, how few pairs of frontier models can be distinguished statistically, and how little IRT test information MMLU carries at the frontier. Code and all intermediate outputs are released. We also give a pre-registered protocol for applying the test to benchmarks whose audits are kept private.

## 1 Introduction

Whether existing benchmarks are *saturated* (scores so close to the attainable ceiling that differences no longer mean anything) or *contaminated* (test items leaked into training data, so scores overstate ability) is one of the central open questions in AI evaluation [Sainz et al., 2023; Ott et al., 2022]. The two diagnoses call for different remedies. A saturated benchmark should be retired or replaced with harder items [Wang et al., 2024; Phan et al., 2025]. A contaminated one should be replaced with fresh or private items [Zhang et al., 2024; White et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024]. In practice both are inferred from the same thing, a high score.

Recent audits make a third factor impossible to ignore: **label noise**. Gema et al. [2025] estimate that 6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors. Vendrow et al. [2025] find that on most of the "saturated" benchmarks they re-annotated, more than half of the remaining model failures trace back to label noise. Label errors interact with both diagnoses. They impose a ceiling below 100%, which matters for saturation. They also create a natural *canary*, which matters for contamination: a model that has memorised a test set should reproduce its wrong keys, while a model that reasons should not. The idea of using wrong labels to detect memorisation is intuitive and is discussed informally in the contamination literature. We are not aware of a treatment that asks what such a test can and cannot identify.

This paper provides that treatment and applies it to the most heavily used saturated benchmark, MMLU [Hendrycks et al., 2021]. The key observation is that the wrong-key test depends on an *audit*, and audits are fallible too. When the audit is wrong, the original key is correct, and stronger models will choose it more often *because* they are stronger. That pattern is exactly the signature one would expect from memorisation that increases with capacity [Carlini et al., 2023].

**Contributions.**

1. **A response model for wrong-key agreement** (Section 3). It separates three mechanisms behind a model choosing a flagged key: the wrong key's plausibility (a distractor's pull, which decreases with ability), audit error (a correct key, whose choice increases with ability), and memorisation (a floor λ). We prove that *item-concentrated* memorisation is observationally equivalent to audit error (Proposition 2), so response data alone cannot measure absolute contamination. We also prove that the naive ability–agreement test is biased towards finding memorisation whenever the audit-error rate exceeds a computable threshold η\*(θ) (Proposition 3). Under *item-homogeneous* memorisation, λ is identified and admits distribution-free per-model bounds (Proposition 4).
2. **An estimator and test.** These are an EM-fitted mixture with class-B response functions constrained to look like correctly keyed items (constraints taken from 5,330 audited-correct items), a likelihood-ratio test calibrated by parametric bootstrap, and profile-likelihood upper bounds on memorisation. A simulation study shows that the naive test's false-positive rate rises with audit error while the mixture test keeps its size (Section 5).
3. **An empirical audit of the audit.** We join 80 HELM MMLU models (per-instance predictions on all 14,015 de-duplicated test items) with the 5,700 MMLU-Redux annotations. The naive test is significant, but the signal comes from items where the audit, not the key, is likely wrong. We confirm this by re-adjudicating the 23 most suspicious items by hand and by numerically verifying the computational ones. After accounting for audit error, the data are consistent with no item-homogeneous memorisation among the 80 models, and the bound on it is tight (Section 6).
4. **Saturation diagnostics on the same data.** These are a label-noise ceiling with uncertainty, the share of frontier errors falling on flagged items, the fraction of frontier model pairs that a paired test can still separate, and IRT test information at the frontier (Section 7). Together they show that MMLU's problem at the top is *measurement resolution*, not detectable memorisation.
5. **A pre-registered protocol** for running the wrong-key test on new benchmarks with private audits. The protocol is designed around the non-identifiability result (Section 8).

We stress what we do *not* claim. Our null result concerns *differential, item-homogeneous* memorisation among the 80 HELM models, measured through 101 flagged items in a 5,700-item audited sample. It does not show that MMLU is uncontaminated. By Proposition 2, it cannot rule out memorisation concentrated on particular items, or memorisation that leaves no trace in multiple-choice selection.

## 2 Related Work

**Contamination detection.** Methods fall into roughly three groups. *Data-access* methods need the training corpus. *Likelihood-based* black-box methods score how likely the model finds test text: Min-K% Prob [Shi et al., 2024], exchangeability tests based on canonical orderings [Oren et al., 2024], and guided-completion probes [Golchin & Surdeanu, 2024]. *Performance-gap* methods compare the original items with fresh or perturbed ones: GSM1k [Zhang et al., 2024], rephrased-sample studies [Yang et al., 2023], option-shuffling and masking probes [Deng et al., 2024], and contamination-free re-collections such as MMLU-CF [Zhao et al., 2024]. Dekoninck et al. [2024] show that many detectors are easy to evade, and Sainz et al. [2023] call for per-benchmark contamination measurement. Wrong-key agreement belongs to a fourth, little-studied group, *outcome-signature* tests that need only the model's answers on the original items. Their appeal is that they work on any public leaderboard log. Our contribution is to establish what such a test can identify.

**Label errors in benchmarks.** Northcutt et al. [2021] estimate that at least 3.3% of test labels are wrong across ten common datasets, and that label errors can destabilise rankings. For MMLU, Gema et al. [2025] release MMLU-Redux, 5,700 re-annotated questions across all 57 subjects, with an error taxonomy. Vendrow et al. [2025] build "platinum" versions of fifteen benchmarks. Rodriguez et al. [2021] use IRT leaderboards to flag annotation errors through items with negative discrimination. We use audits as an *instrument* and ask how audit error propagates into contamination inference. Questions about the audit labels themselves have also been raised publicly, for example a GitHub issue disputing an international-law correction in MMLU-Redux [aryopg/mmlu-redux issue #8]. Our item-level analysis finds the same item.

**Saturation and measurement.** Ott et al. [2022] map benchmark creation and saturation dynamics across 3,765 benchmarks. Statistical treatments of evaluation stress paired designs and error bars [Miller, 2024] and quantify seed and benchmark variance [Madaan et al., 2024]. IRT has been used to compress benchmarks [Polo et al., 2024] and to weight items by informativeness [Rodriguez et al., 2021]. Latent-class models for unknown ground truth go back to Dawid & Skene [1979]. Our mixture is a two-class item-level analogue in which the "annotator" is the audit and the "observers" are models with known ability.

## 3 A Response Model for Wrong-Key Agreement

**Setup.** A multiple-choice benchmark has items i, each with an original key g_i. An audit flags a subset ℱ of items as having a wrong key and supplies a corrected answer t_i ≠ g_i. Let Z_i ∈ {A, B} denote whether the audit is right (A: g_i is wrong) or wrong (B: g_i is in fact correct), with P(Z_i = B) = η. Models m = 1, …, M have a scalar ability θ_m, estimated from items the audit marked as correct. Let Y_mi = 1 if model m chooses g_i. Conditional on θ and the item, responses are independent across models.

**Clean response functions.** Without memorisation, write P_i^A(θ) = P(Y_mi = 1 | Z_i = A, θ_m = θ), the pull of a wrong option, and P_i^B(θ) = P(Y_mi = 1 | Z_i = B, θ), the probability of answering a correctly keyed item correctly. We assume:

* **(A1) Plausibility is non-increasing in ability.** P_i^A is non-increasing.
* **(A2) Competence is non-decreasing in ability.** P_i^B is non-decreasing (the standard IRT monotonicity assumption).

A2 is the defining assumption of IRT. A1 can be checked empirically. Among the 3,605 audited-correct MMLU-Redux items on which at least three models chose the same distractor, the logistic slope of choosing that most popular distractor against ability is negative for 80.3% of items (median slope −0.65; `results/theory_quantities.json`).

**Memorisation.** With probability λ_mi, model m outputs the stored key regardless of competence:
P(Y_mi = 1) = λ_mi + (1 − λ_mi) P_i^{Z_i}(θ_m).
Memorisation is *item-homogeneous* if λ_mi = λ(θ_m) on all class-A items, and *item-concentrated* otherwise. (On class-B items memorisation cannot be told apart from competence and does not distort accuracy, so we define it on class-A items.)

**Proposition 1 (label-noise ceiling).** Partition items into audit categories c with population shares ρ_c, and let κ_c be the probability that the true answer coincides with the original key in category c (κ_ok = 1 and κ_wrong-key = η). An oracle that always picks the true answer has expected accuracy Acc\* = Σ_c ρ_c κ_c with respect to the original keys. A model with expected accuracy above Acc\* must agree with original keys beyond the truth on flagged items.
*Proof.* Expected accuracy is linear in category shares. The oracle agrees with the key exactly when the truth coincides with it. ∎
Acc\* depends on η and on the κ_c of ambiguous categories (bad clarity, multiple correct answers, no correct answer, expert-only), none of which is known a priori. We therefore report a central estimate and bounds (Section 7).

**Proposition 2 (item-concentrated memorisation is not identifiable).** Let class-B response functions be unrestricted within the non-decreasing functions 𝒫↑. For any class-A item with memorisation profile λ_i(θ) such that Q_i(θ) = λ_i(θ) + (1 − λ_i(θ)) P_i^A(θ) is non-decreasing, there is a class-B item with P_i^B = Q_i and no memorisation that induces the same joint distribution of (Y_1i, …, Y_Mi) for every set of abilities. Conversely, a class-B item with P_i^B is reproduced by a class-A item with λ_i(θ) = (P_i^B(θ) − P_i^A(θ)) / (1 − P_i^A(θ)) wherever this lies in [0, 1].
*Proof.* Given θ, responses are independent Bernoulli(Q_i(θ_m)) in both cases, and Q_i ∈ 𝒫↑ by hypothesis. The converse follows by solving λ + (1 − λ)P^A = P^B for λ. ∎
*Consequence.* From responses alone, "the audit is wrong on item i" and "models memorised item i in proportion to their capacity" are the same hypothesis. Any contamination claim built on wrong-key agreement therefore needs either (a) independent verification of the audit on the items that drive the signal, or (b) a cross-item restriction such as homogeneity. This remains true with infinitely many models.

**Proposition 3 (sign bias of the naive test).** Let Ā(θ) and B̄(θ) be the class-averaged clean response functions, and assume no memorisation. The population mean agreement W̄(θ) = (1 − η) Ā(θ) + η B̄(θ) satisfies dW̄/dθ > 0 if and only if
η > η\*(θ) := |Ā′(θ)| / (|Ā′(θ)| + B̄′(θ)).
*Proof.* Differentiate and use Ā′ ≤ 0 ≤ B̄′ (A1, A2). ∎
*Consequence.* "Stronger models agree more with the flagged keys" is evidence of memorisation only if the audit-error rate is known to lie below η\*. Since η\* depends on how sharply competence rises relative to how fast plausibility falls, it can be small for discriminating items.

**Proposition 4 (identification and bounds under homogeneity).** Under item-homogeneous memorisation and A1:
(i) for every class-A item, λ(θ) ≤ P(Y_mi = 1 | θ_m = θ). Hence if model m chose the flagged key on k_m of n_m class-A items, the one-sided Clopper–Pearson bound ū(k_m, n_m) is a valid 95% upper bound on λ(θ_m), whatever the shape of P_i^A.
(ii) In the parametric family below, λ(·) is identified as a floor shared by all class-A items, distinct from the item-specific class-B curves.
*Proof.* (i) P(Y = 1) = λ + (1 − λ)P^A ≥ λ, and the n_m items give independent Bernoulli draws with success probability at least λ, so the count stochastically dominates Binomial(n_m, λ). (ii) follows because a common additive floor cannot be absorbed into item-specific parameters across items whose P_i^A → 0 differently. ∎
Bound (i) assumes the class labels are right. In practice we apply it to items with posterior P(Z = B) < 0.5, which adds a dependence on the model.

## 4 Estimation

**Parametric family.** Write σ for the logistic function and θ for standardised ability. For each flagged item:

* class B: P_i^B(θ) = σ(a_i + s(b_i) θ), where s is the softplus (so the slope is ≥ 0);
* class A: P_i(θ) = λ(θ) + (1 − λ(θ)) σ(c_i − s(d_i) θ), with λ(θ) = σ(γ_0 + γ_1 θ).

The null hypothesis H₀: λ ≡ 0 sits on the boundary of the parameter space.

**Constraining class B to look correctly keyed.** Without constraints, a flagged item that every model avoids can be "explained" as a very hard class-B item. We therefore fit per-item logistic curves P(correct | θ) to the 5,330 MMLU-Redux items marked OK. We require class-B items to have an intercept at least the 5th percentile of OK-item intercepts (a_i ≥ −1.72) and a slope s(b_i) ≥ 0.2 (`results/ok_item_params.json`). In words, an item can be called an audit error only if models answer it the way they answer correctly keyed MMLU items.

**Fitting and testing.** We maximise the marginal likelihood by EM, with the posterior r_i = P(Z_i = B | Y) as the E-step and per-item L-BFGS-B fits plus a pooled fit of (γ_0, γ_1) as the M-step. The memorisation model is initialised at the null solution, so the LR statistic is non-negative. The LR is calibrated by a parametric bootstrap: 60 replicates drawn from the fitted null, with each item's class drawn from its posterior. We bound λ by profiling the likelihood over fixed λ, (a) constant across all models and (b) applied only to the 20 strongest models, using a one-sided 95% cut-off (deviance 2.71).

**Ability.** θ_m is the standardised logit accuracy of model m on the OK items of the Redux sample (5,330 items). It is therefore computed from items disjoint from ℱ.
